Sailing the Solent. A skipper's mental-rehearsal brief for David and Lobke.

Welcome. This is your audio brief for the Isle of Wight sailing trip — something to listen to on a walk or in the car, so that by the time you step aboard, the whole thing already feels familiar. It's in three parts. First, the trip in a nutshell. Second, and this is the meat of it, a mental rehearsal of the actual skippering. And third, where things stand and what happens next. None of it is hard. The point of listening is simply to make the handful of moments that matter feel automatic.

Let's start with the shape of the trip.

The plan is a couple's sailing holiday — just you and Lobke, with Noah staying with his grandparents. You're chartering a yacht of around thirty-seven feet, a comfortable size that two people can handle easily, and sailing what's known as a taster route through the Solent and around the eastern side of the Isle of Wight. Think a midweek trip, three or four days, aimed at the twenty twenty-seven season — June or early September, when the weather is settled and the water is quieter than the peak of summer.

Here's the reassuring bit. You already hold everything the job needs. Your Day Skipper practical qualification, gained in tidal waters, is exactly what British charter companies ask for, and you also hold the V-H-F short-range radio certificate, which several of them want alongside it. Lobke is Day-Skipper-trained and a thoroughly competent second pair of hands, which clears the requirement that there be at least one capable crew member besides the skipper. So this is a straightforward two-handed bareboat charter. No professional skipper required, no extra courses to sit. Two ticket-holders on a small yacht, in sheltered water, in good weather. This is about the gentlest bareboat sailing there is.

The route never leaves protected water. You never round the Needles, you never take on an open-sea passage. Every leg is a short hop of two to four hours, and there is always a safe harbour within easy reach. The classic flow runs from your home marina across to Cowes on the first day — an easy shake-down sail to loosen up. Then west along the island to Yarmouth, a pretty and well-sheltered little harbour. Then to either the Beaulieu River, up to Buckler's Hard, or the secluded anchorage at Newtown Creek for a quiet night at anchor. And finally back to base. A gentle, rewarding little cruise.

On cost, a midweek charter of this size runs somewhere around eleven hundred to fifteen hundred pounds, plus fuel, food, and marina fees of a couple of hundred more, plus a refundable security deposit held against damage. There's a shortlist of three good operators to get quotes from, with a lead pick that has the cleanest terms and the lowest deposit. And getting there from Belgium is easy — the Eurostar to London and a direct train down to the coast, or the tunnel and your own car if you'd rather carry all the kit.

That's the trip. Now for the part that's actually worth rehearsing — the skippering.

Here's how to use what follows. Do it in an armchair, eyes shut, the week before. Pilots call it chair-flying. Don't just summarise the plan in your head — narrate the actions, in order, out loud if you can. Engine on. Checks done. Brief Lobke. Check astern. Feel the tide setting you off the pontoon, and let it work for you. Speaking the sequence aloud is what fixes the order in your mind. And whenever you rehearse something going wrong, always rehearse your response to it, and then stop there. You're training the calm reaction, not the fear.

There are five set-pieces worth rehearsing until they're reflex. Everything else on this route is relaxed. These are the five.

The first is leaving and returning to the berth, with just the two of you. This is the moment most likely to feel fiddly, so make it a system. Before you touch a line, read the three forces acting on the boat. Which way is the wind pushing you. Which way is the tide setting on the berth. And which way does your propeller kick the stern when you go astern — you can find that out with a short burst of reverse while you're still tied on. Once you know those three things, set your lines to work with them, not against them. If the tide will carry your bow off the pontoon, let it — slip everything but a stern line and peel out backwards. If the wind is pinning you on, rig a spring line, motor gently against it to swing one end out, and then slip. Fenders and lines ready to recover, Lobke on the last line, you on the helm, and a good look all around before you move, because Solent marinas are busy places.

One note on that first move. If you charter from the Portsmouth base, the lead pick, your very first task after leaving the marina is the Portsmouth Harbour entrance — narrow, strong tide, and shared with commercial ships and ferries. Keep to the small-boat channel on the western side, listen to the harbour master on the radio, and never get in the way of a big ship or the ferry. If instead you sail from the Hamble or Lymington, your exit is a gentle motor down a river, which is easier, though you then cross a bit more open water.

The second set-piece is reading the tide, because in the Solent the tide is your engine. The streams here run hard — two or three knots in the main channel, more at the edges. So plan every single leg to carry a fair tide. Waiting an hour for the tide to turn in your favour can be worth a knot and a half of free speed, and it turns a slog into a glide. The Solent also has an unusually long, flat top to its high water — a double high water — and that long stand gives you a generous window to slip into the shallow harbours. Which brings us to the tidal gates. Two of your possible stops, the Beaulieu River bar and Newtown Creek, are shallow, and you'll want to arrive with enough height of tide to get in safely. So one of your jobs before those legs is a quick tidal-height calculation. Nothing elaborate — the rule of twelves in your head is plenty. But do it, and arrive on a rising tide with water to spare.

The third set-piece is crossing the shipping channel cleanly. The Solent is one of the busiest stretches of water in the world. Large commercial ships use the marked channel in and out of Southampton, and they have what's called a moving prohibited zone around them — small craft must keep well clear, roughly a thousand metres ahead and a hundred metres to the side. So the rule is simple. Don't loiter in the channel. Cross it at right angles, briskly, in a clear gap between ships. Keep an eye out for the fast ferries, which will not stop for you, and for the little chain ferry that crosses the river at Cowes. And know where the Bramble Bank sits, right in the middle of the Solent — it dries out near low water, so much so that they've been known to play a game of cricket on it. The marks and the channel keep you clear of it.

The fourth set-piece is coming alongside, and picking up a mooring buoy, short-handed. The trick that makes berthing a non-event with only two of you is the midships line. Have your fenders and lines rigged early. Approach slowly, into whichever is stronger, the wind or the tide. Get one line from the middle of the boat onto a cleat on the pontoon first, and motor gently ahead against it — the boat pins herself neatly alongside, and then the two of you can step off and make fast the bow and stern at your leisure. For a mooring buoy, approach into the wind or tide, aim to stop with the buoy right by the shrouds, and have Lobke pick it up with the boathook and lead the strop to a cleat. And if you miss it — everyone misses one sometimes — just circle round calmly and try again. No drama. For anchoring, pick your spot for swinging room and good holding, drop with the bow into the wind or tide, pay out about four times the depth in chain, and take a transit on something ashore to check she's holding before you settle down for the evening.

The fifth set-piece is man overboard, with only two of you aboard. This is the one genuine what-if, and mostly it's a prevention drill. Wear your lifejackets, always, and clip on to the safety lines when you're on deck or when it's lively, and especially at night. Prevention is nine-tenths of it. But do rehearse the recovery, because with two people it's demanding. If someone goes over, the immediate actions are: shout, hit the man-overboard button on the radio or the chart plotter to mark the spot, throw the danbuoy and horseshoe, and never take your eyes off them — one of you points, continuously, and does nothing else. Then bring the boat back under engine, stop alongside them to leeward, engine into neutral as you come close, and get a line onto them. The hard part, with only one person left to pull, is getting them back aboard — so think it through in advance: a halyard led to a winch, the boarding ladder, a bight of rope as a step. Rehearse that sequence until it's automatic. It's the one drill that genuinely earns its place.

A few quick contingencies beyond those five. If the engine fails, remember you have sails — bear away into open water with room around you, then diagnose it calmly; if you can't fix it, sail onto a mooring or anchor rather than drifting into the channel. If fog comes down, slow right down, put your navigation lights on, make your sound signals, keep to the edge of the channel out of the ships' water, and use the plotter. If you touch the bottom, the Solent is mostly soft mud, so don't just open the throttle — check the tide first. If it's rising, you'll likely float off, so kedge or wait. If it's falling, act quickly: engine astern, heel the boat over to reduce how deep she sits, and motor back the way you came.

And Lobke's role, so nobody is left guessing. Agree it all in advance. Which line she takes leaving and arriving, and the midships-first plan alongside. The foredeck jobs — the anchor, the mooring buoy, any headsail work — always clipped on. She calls the traffic and the ferries while you helm the channel crossing. And if it's you who goes over the side, her one job is to point and not lose sight of you while she works the radio and the engine. Worth one dry run together. Beyond that, she runs the kettle and the pilot book, and you run the boat.

That's the rehearsal. Run it a few times in the armchair and none of it will feel new when you're aboard.
